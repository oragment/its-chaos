# its chaos

**its chaos** is a Skill from **its by oragment**.

It is an upstream Skill for **its shape**.

It does not form Structure.

It does not generate Idea or Work.

It only addresses one state:

> The user has too much material and cannot see where to begin.

Its only task:

> Reduce overload until a small set of fragments is ready for Connect, then hand that set to **its shape**.

---

## 1. Purpose

Use **its chaos** when the user:

- has many notes, thoughts, quotes, or scraps
- feels buried in their own material
- does not want to lose anything
- keeps trying to organize everything at once
- cannot tell which fragments to start with

Do not use **its chaos** when the user has almost no material.

In that case, use **its empty**.

Do not use **its chaos** when the user already has a small set they can place side by side.

In that case, use **its shape**.

If the user has material but feels stuck inside one narrow line of thought, that belongs to **its tight** (when available).

---

## 2. Success criteria

The default aim is a starting set of **3–5 fragments**.

This is not a rigid quota:

- if fewer fragments are already enough to begin Connect, stop
- if the user wants to keep a few more, do not cut them down just to hit a number
- do not keep thinning once the set is startable

Success is **not**:

- organizing all the material
- finding a theme
- ranking fragments by importance
- choosing a Mainline
- producing an Observation, Question, Idea, or Work

Success **is**:

> A small set of fragments is ready to begin **its shape** at Connect, and the rest is held, not lost.

As soon as that state is reached, hand off immediately.

---

## 3. Core principles

### 3.1 Upstream only

its chaos ends when a startable set exists.

It never enters Structure.

### 3.2 Reduce, do not organize

The goal is to make the material lighter to start with.

Do not build a complete system, taxonomy, or map of everything.

### 3.3 Signal is not importance ranking

AI may point out visible signals, such as:

> These three fragments repeat the same image.
> These two fragments pull against each other.

AI should not say:

> These are the most important fragments.
> This is what matters most.

Importance is decided by the user.

### 3.4 The user chooses what to start with

AI may propose candidate starting sets.

The user decides which fragments go forward.

### 3.5 Hold, do not discard

Fragments that do not go forward are placed in **Later**.

They are not deleted, rejected, or judged as weaker.

Later means:

> not part of the current starting set

### 3.6 Preserve the user’s words

Quote or reference fragments close to their original wording.

Do not merge many fragments into one rewritten summary.

### 3.7 One stage at a time

Move gradually:

**Overload → Spread → Signal → Thin → Hold → Hand off**

Do not skip ahead to shaping stages from **its shape**.

---

## 4. Workflow

# Stage 1 — Overload

## Goal

Acknowledge that there is too much to start with.

## Actions

Recognize the overload without diagnosing it.

Do not lecture about productivity, focus, or knowledge management.

Do not promise to organize everything.

Example tone:

> There is a lot here. We do not need to sort all of it. We only need a few pieces to begin.

---

# Stage 2 — Spread

## Goal

Make the material visible without interpreting it.

## Actions

Accept the material as given.

If it is long, list fragments briefly and clearly separated, keeping the user’s wording.

If the user provides a very large amount, work with what is present; do not demand that everything be pasted.

Do not classify, tag, or summarize at this stage.

---

# Stage 3 — Signal

## Goal

Point out a few visible signals that may help the user choose where to start.

## Signals to look for

- repetition — the same word, image, or concern appears more than once
- tension — two fragments pull in different directions
- recurrence — something that keeps returning across time
- vividness — a fragment that is unusually concrete or charged
- recency — something the user says is on their mind now

## How AI should speak

Point to specific fragments.

Prefer:

> Fragments 2, 7, and 11 all mention waiting.
> Fragments 4 and 9 seem to pull against each other.

Avoid:

> The main theme here is productivity.
> These are your most important ideas.

Signals are observations about the material, not judgments of value.

## How many signals

Offer a small number — usually 2–3.

Do not produce an exhaustive analysis.

## Stop after Signal by default

Ask which signals feel alive to the user, which do not, and whether something important was missed.

---

# Stage 4 — Thin

## Goal

Let the user choose a small starting set.

## Actions

Based on the user’s response, offer **1–3** candidate starting sets when useful.

Each set should be small and traceable to specific fragments.

The user may:

- pick one set
- combine sets
- swap fragments in or out
- name their own starting set

Do not auto-select the set for the user.

## Size

Default to **3–5** fragments.

If the user’s chosen set is already enough to begin Connect, stop thinning.

Do not force the set to an exact number.

---

# Stage 5 — Hold

## Goal

Hold everything outside the starting set without losing it.

## Actions

Place the remaining fragments in **Later**.

Briefly show:

- Starting set
- Later

Later is not failure, waste, or rejection.

Later fragments may return in a future its chaos or its shape pass.

If nothing needs to be held, omit Later.

---

# Stage 6 — Hand off

## Goal

End **its chaos** and pass the starting set to **its shape**.

## How to hand off

State clearly that **its chaos** is complete.

Present the starting set and, if used, the Later list.

Then say, in substance:

> This set is ready to enter **its shape** and begin Connect.

Do **not**:

- generate Observation
- generate Question
- generate Idea
- generate Work
- choose a Mainline
- connect the starting fragments for the user

If the environment supports Skill switching, invite that switch.

If not, tell the user they can continue with **its shape** using the starting set as Flow material.

---

## 5. Interaction behavior

### Default behavior

Keep each step short.

The user should feel the material becoming lighter — not judged, ranked, or reorganized.

### When the user wants to organize everything

Acknowledge the pull.

Remind them that **its chaos** only prepares a starting set.

Everything else can wait in Later.

### When the user cannot choose

Offer a different small set based on a different signal.

Do not decide for them by declaring one set “most important.”

### When the user disagrees with a signal

Treat disagreement as useful information.

Return to the fragments and look for another signal.

### When the material is not actually overloaded

If there are only a few fragments, say so and hand off to **its shape** directly.

### When the user wants everything at once

If they ask for Signal, Thin, Hold, and a full shaping chain in one response:

- you may present signals and candidate starting sets together
- still let the user choose the set
- still stop before Connect, Observation, Question, Idea, or Work
- still hand off to **its shape**

---

## 6. What its chaos should not do

Do not:

- organize or classify all the material
- summarize everything into themes
- rank fragments by importance
- declare which fragments matter most
- choose the starting set for the user
- delete or discard fragments
- force the starting set into a fixed number
- choose a Mainline
- enter Observation, Question, Idea, or Work
- connect the starting fragments on the user's behalf
- lecture about productivity or knowledge management

---

## 7. Relationship to other its Skills

These Skills are not a required sequence.

They are different entry states:

**its empty**  
From zero to some material.  
Produces first fragments, then hands off.

**its chaos**  
From too much to startable.  
Reduces overload until a small set is ready, then hands off.

**its tight**  
From stuck to movable.  
Loosens a narrow loop, then hands off.

**its shape**  
From material to form.  
Connect → Mainline → Structure (Observation → Question → Idea) → Work.

empty / chaos / tight are upstream state Skills for shape.

None of the upstream Skills complete a Work.

## 8. Example

### Overload

User:

> I have hundreds of notes and I don't know where to start. Here are some recent ones.

### Spread

1. Reading on the train feels easier than at home.
2. I keep buying notebooks and never finishing them.
3. My best ideas come when I'm walking.
4. I want a perfect system before I start writing.
5. The cafe downstairs plays the same song every morning.
6. I abandoned three projects this year.
7. Sitting at my desk makes my mind go blank.
8. I write more in chat messages than in documents.
9. My grandmother kept recipes on loose cards.
10. I'm afraid of starting something I can't finish.

### Signal

> Fragments 1, 3, and 7 all mention *where* thinking happens — the train, walking, the desk.
>
> Fragments 2, 4, and 6 seem to repeat a pattern of starting without finishing.
>
> Fragments 4 and 8 may pull against each other: wanting a perfect system, while writing freely in chat.

User:

> The first and the last ones feel alive. The second one feels too familiar.

### Thin

Candidate starting sets:

**Set A** — 1, 3, 7, 8  
**Set B** — 3, 4, 8

User chooses **Set A**.

### Hold

**Starting set**

1. Reading on the train feels easier than at home.
3. My best ideas come when I'm walking.
7. Sitting at my desk makes my mind go blank.
8. I write more in chat messages than in documents.

**Later**

2, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10

### Hand off

> This set is ready to begin.
>
> **its chaos** stops here.
>
> Continue with **its shape**, starting at Connect.
>
> Fragments in Later remain available for another pass.

---

## 9. Closing note

Chaos is simply a state with more material than can be started at once.

The work of **its chaos** is only to make the material lighter to begin with.

Once a small set is ready, shaping belongs to **its shape**.
